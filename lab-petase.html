Wet-Lab PETase Engineering

Validating my computationally designed FAST-PETase variants at GenSpace, a community bio lab in Brooklyn

Project Overview

This is the wet-lab continuation of my computational PETase work. Previously, I computationally engineered disulfide bonds into FAST-PETase and used molecular dynamics simulations to show improved thermal stability. After emailing nearly 50 labs, I found a home at GenSpace in Brooklyn, where I'm bringing those designs into the lab: expressing and purifying FAST-PETase and a disulfide-engineered variant in E. coli, confirming the engineered bond, and directly measuring whether the variant is more thermostable and degrades PET faster or at higher temperatures than the control.

Background

  • PETase (from Ideonella sakaiensis) breaks PET into MHET, which MHETase further degrades to terephthalic acid and ethylene glycol — a biocatalytic recycling pathway.
  • FAST-PETase (Functional, Active, Stable, Tolerant) carries five mutations vs. wild-type and can nearly fully degrade untreated post-consumer PET in about a week at 50 °C — but loses function above 50 °C.
  • My computational variants added engineered disulfide bonds at thermally weak regions, and MD simulations predicted improved stability. This project tests those predictions experimentally.

Approach

  • Express & purify the control: transform the FAST-PETase plasmid (pET21b, C-terminal His-tag) into E. coli BL21(DE3), induce with IPTG, and purify by affinity chromatography.
  • Build the variant: introduce the disulfide mutation by site-directed mutagenesis, then express and purify it the same way.
  • Confirm the bond: verify that the engineered disulfide formed by comparing the two proteins on SDS-PAGE.
  • Measure stability: determine the melting temperature (Tm) of both proteins by differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) and compare.
  • Test function: incubate both with amorphous PET film across a temperature range and measure product release by HPLC to see whether the variant degrades PET faster or at higher temperatures.

Starting Experiments

  • Experiment 1 — Expression & purification: can I successfully express and purify FAST-PETase in BL21(DE3)? Transform by heat shock, control antibiotic and IPTG concentration, induction temperature, and lysis method; assess by growth curve, SDS-PAGE (band size and purity), and protein concentration.
  • Experiment 2 — Stability comparison: once both proteins are in hand, compare their melting temperatures by DSF to test whether the engineered disulfide raises Tm.

Key Considerations

  • Induction optimization: lowering IPTG concentration and temperature reduces expression pressure and improves soluble yield.
  • Disulfide formation: the BL21 cytoplasm reduces disulfide bonds while the periplasm allows them to form — a real constraint for a disulfide-engineered enzyme.
  • Substrate choice: labs use amorphous PET because it's more accessible to the enzyme than the crystalline PET in bottles.

Tools & Equipment

  • Recombinant expression: pET21b plasmid, E. coli BL21(DE3), IPTG induction
  • Growth & lysis: LB broth/agar, antibiotic, incubator, OD600 reader, centrifuge, sonicator
  • Purification: affinity resin and columns
  • Analysis: SDS-PAGE, differential scanning fluorimetry (Tm), HPLC (product release)

Constituting Education

A computational text-analysis study of what national education policy says schools are for

Project Overview

Every country writes down, somewhere, what it believes schools are for. This project applies computational text analysis to national education policy documents from roughly ten countries to test a single question: has the language of human capital — skills, workforce readiness, economic productivity — displaced older humanistic, civic, and religious conceptions of the purpose of education? The output is a publication-quality research paper (~8–10k words) built on a corpus I constructed from scratch.

The Central Question

Since roughly the 1980s, market language — human capital, "college-and-career readiness," competitiveness — appears to have crowded out older languages of humanistic flourishing, civic virtue, and religious formation. The United States is the clearest case. The question this project asks is whether that shift is universal, by measuring how much weight each conception carries across countries and over time.

Theoretical Framework

The analytic spine is Biesta's three functions of education:

  • Qualification ≈ human capital — "preparing students for the workforce."
  • Socialization ≈ civic / religious — "instilling values, national identity, tradition."
  • Subjectification ≈ flourishing — "developing autonomous, curious, creative persons."

Tracking the relative weight of these three, per country and over time, is the argument.

Building the Corpus

  • No off-the-shelf dataset exists — I am constructing it: ~10–13 documents, one primary per country, all the same genre (national curriculum framework, with national education policy/act as a fallback).
  • The genre rule is critical: comparing a curriculum framework to a sector financing plan compares ideology to logistics, not ideology to ideology.
  • Wave 1 (clean English pilot): US, Finland, Singapore, South Africa, Kenya, India, Afghanistan, UK, Norway, Rwanda.
  • Wave 2 (translation-gated): China, Brazil, Iran — added once the pipeline is validated.
  • Each primary document is logged with source, issuing body, year, language, and a metadata table (corpus_meta.csv) carrying covariates like region, regime score (V-Dem), income level (World Bank), and legal tradition.

Method

  • Pre-register the research question, directional hypothesis, genre rule, and four seed keyword lists before modeling — an anti-confirmation-bias gate.
  • Extract text from each primary PDF (pdfplumber / pdftools), with OCR where needed, isolating aims and purpose passages.
  • Preprocess into a document-feature matrix (lowercasing, boilerplate stripping, domain stopwords, lemmatization, key bigrams) at both whole-document and section level.
  • Model in three passes: STM with covariates (unsupervised discovery), keyATM with four seed lists (theory-directed), and BERTopic at section level (embedding-based robustness check).
  • Map the resulting topics back to Biesta's three functions with a two-coder reliability check, then analyze prevalence by covariate and trace the US temporal arc (1918 → 1965 → 1983 → 1994 → 2001 → 2010).

Tools & Technologies

  • Python (pdfplumber) and R (pdftools) — text extraction
  • STM — structural topic modeling with covariates
  • keyATM — seeded, theory-directed topic modeling
  • BERTopic — embedding-based topic modeling for triangulation
  • V-Dem, World Bank, and JuriGlobe — covariate data sources

What I'm Learning

  • How to design a defensible comparative corpus, where genre control matters as much as the modeling.
  • How pre-registration guards a text-analysis study against confirmation bias.
  • How to triangulate unsupervised, seeded, and embedding-based topic models to make a finding robust rather than an artifact of one method.
  • How to connect a computational result back to a genuine argument in the philosophy of education.

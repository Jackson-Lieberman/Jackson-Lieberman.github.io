Lignin as Sustainable Jet Fuel

Oxygen Content vs. Energy Density: A Calorimetric Study of Lignin Conversion to Jet Fuel · UPenn Chemistry Research Academy · 2026

📄 Published in Spectral Insights, Vol. 1 (UPSD Press, 2026) · Presented at the UPenn Chemistry Symposium

Project Overview

Lignin, the polymer that makes wood rigid, is the most abundant renewable source of aromatic hydrocarbons on Earth, which makes it a promising feedstock for sustainable aviation fuel. But it is about 30% oxygen by weight, and it can't be burned as fuel directly. We asked how a fuel's oxygen content affects its energy, and what that reveals about why lignin has to be deoxygenated before it can become jet fuel.

Methods

  • Burned seven materials spanning 18–53 wt% oxygen: cotton, almonds, Cool Ranch Doritos, Flamin' Hot Cheetos, sucrose, glucose, and Kraft lignin.
  • Measured energy density by calorimetry: ~2.00 g samples burned 4 cm beneath 50 mL of water, with energy density = Q ÷ mass burned.
  • Ran ATR-FTIR spectroscopy on lignin to identify its aromatic rings and oxygen-containing groups.
  • Used regression to relate oxygen content to energy density.

Key Results

  • Energy density rose steadily as oxygen content fell (R² = 0.78): about 1.1 kJ/g gained per 10 percentage points of oxygen removed.
  • Oxygen content alone didn't decide whether something burned: lignin (28.9 wt% O) would not ignite, while cotton (49.3 wt% O) burned readily.
  • FTIR showed lignin has the aromatic rings jet fuel needs (~1600 and ~1510 cm⁻¹) alongside O–H, methoxyl, and C–O groups that block clean combustion.
  • Conclusion: lignin-to-jet-fuel needs two distinct steps, depolymerization to break the polymer and hydrodeoxygenation to remove oxygen, because neither problem alone explains why lignin fails as a direct fuel.
